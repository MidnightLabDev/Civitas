# Operational psychohistory | research inspired method, not a predictive science

## The central question

Given changing incentives, institutions, resources, technology, beliefs, communication networks and shocks, **which collective futures become more or less plausible under explicit assumptions, and what can decision makers test now?**

This question is narrower and defensible compared with claiming to predict historical destiny. Treat the term "psychohistory" as literary inspiration for an original **synthetic systems methodology**.

## Ten building blocks

1. **Units:** households, firms, groups, institutions, autonomous agents, polities or simulated societies. Every aggregation is a choice, not an objective fact.
2. **Heterogeneity:** distinguish actors by resources, preferences, access, constraints, trust, and influence. Do not use a single representative person when differences drive the result.
3. **Macro constraints:** geography, infrastructure, ecological capacity, technology, historical legacies, legal boundaries and contact networks.
4. **State:** explicit variables such as trust, available goods, knowledge, inequality, legitimacy, security or health of a fictional society. Choose operational definitions.
5. **Transitions:** rules for how a state changes over time. Ask whether the rule is observed, assumed or generated by an LLM.
6. **Agency:** actors learn, innovate, misread signals, refuse coordination, form coalitions, exit or change the institution itself.
7. **Interactions:** repeated cooperation, competition, peer effects, strategic anticipation, network spillovers and collective action.
8. **Nonlinear shifts:** thresholds, cascades, tipping behavior, lock in, delayed responses, hysteresis and rare discontinuities, if plausibly modeled.
9. **Reflexivity:** publishing a forecast may change behavior and invalidate the predicted process. Never hide plans from affected people as a supposed real world requirement of effective psychohistory.
10. **Measurement:** observable patterns, uncertainty, competing models, independent data, sensitivity and conditions that would refute a claim.

## The CIVITAS hypothesis ledger

For every significant claim, record: ID, claim, evidence tier (E1 established, E2 evidenced, E3 modeled, E4 exploratory, E5 fiction, E6 speculative; see SKILL.md section 2), mechanism, expected direction, competing explanation, data source, confounders, stress case, falsification and review date. Avoid numeric precision when no data support it.

A useful chain is:

`driver → incentive → actor behavior → interaction → feedback → emergent outcome → observable signal`

Example (a **hypothesis**, not a proven relationship): subsidized shared infrastructure → reduced cost of participation → more smaller firms join → distributed innovation improves → platform network strengthens. A competing mechanism is that shared infrastructure creates monopoly dependence.

## Population regularities versus individuality

Group statistics do not determine particular lives. Beware ecological fallacy (inferring individual characteristics from group averages), atomistic fallacy (generalizing individuals to societies), selection bias, survivorship bias and Simpson's paradox. Population size does not by itself confer accurate predictions. Structural change can break every previously observed relationship.

## Three different purposes

- **Explanation:** Explain how a mechanism could have produced observed history; requires historical corroboration and alternatives.
- **Exploration:** Map plausible consequences if proposed rules operated; can use synthetic simulation and imaginative shocks.
- **Prediction:** Forecast observable outcomes before seeing them; requires real data, preregistered targets or recorded forecasts, scoring, and calibration.

Never present successful exploration as a proven prediction. Model output is a statement about the model conditional on its assumptions.

## Reflection prompts

- What happens if people know the prediction?
- Which unseen minority bears an unmodeled cost?
- Could different rules produce the same observation?
- Is a rare catastrophic outcome masked by a good mean?
- Is the environment being changed by the policy being evaluated?
- Can the model be simpler while retaining its explanatory purpose?
- Does it reproduce evidence from periods it was not fitted to?
- Is the objective itself being contested by the population?

## What real population-scale prediction can and cannot do (added in 1.0 enhancement)

| Can (with evidence) | Cannot |
|---|---|
| Aggregate regularities: mortality, fertility transitions, diffusion S-curves [E1] | Name the year a specific state collapses |
| Elevated-risk assessments for instability onset (Goldstone et al. 2010) [E2] | Predict triggers, leaders, or outcomes of crises |
| Conditional projections under explicit models [E3] | Survive structural breaks the model never saw |
| Calibrated short-horizon probabilities from trained forecasters (Mellers et al. 2014) [E2] | Deliver millennium-scale plans that stay valid |

**Why molecules are a bad analogy**: people anticipate, imitate, organize, and rewrite rules; their interactions create heavy tails (Granovetter thresholds, Kuran's hidden preferences); forecasts feed back (Merton, Lucas). Large numbers help only when individual deviations are independent, which social contagion breaks.

**CIVITAS's replacement for psychohistory**: the CIVITAS Loop (ENGINE.md): conditional mechanisms + base rates + branches + stress tests + early warning + scored forecasts. Its claim is modest and testable: better-calibrated judgment and more robust decisions, not destiny.
