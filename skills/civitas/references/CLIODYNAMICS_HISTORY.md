## 1. Ground rules

- **No single trajectory.** Societies do not climb one ladder or follow one cycle. "Collapse" often means political fragmentation or simplification while populations and cultures continue (McAnany & Yoffee 2010).
- **Multiple causes, interacting.** Climate, disease, fiscal stress, elite conflict, war, trade shifts and ideas usually combine. Single-cause stories are a red flag.
- **Regional breadth.** Draw analogues from at least two regions and eras; avoid treating Rome as the template for everything.
- **Sources disagree.** Present the leading interpretations and what evidence would separate them. Archaeological, textual and paleoclimate evidence each have biases.
- **Tier discipline.** Dates and well-attested events: E1. Causal interpretations: usually E2 with debate. Grand theories: E2 to E4 depending on testing.

## 2. Theories (with critiques)

| Theory | Core mechanism | Strength | Main critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ibn Khaldun's asabiyyah cycle** (*Muqaddimah*, 1377) | Group solidarity forged at frontiers lets a group seize power; luxury and dependence erode it over generations, inviting replacement | Early, durable insight into cohesion and dynastic turnover; formalized by Turchin (2003) | Hard to measure solidarity; risk of romanticizing "hardy" peoples |
| **Structural-demographic theory** (Goldstone 1991; Turchin & Nefedov 2009) | Population growth depresses wages, enriches elites; elite numbers outgrow positions (elite overproduction); state fiscal distress; together these raise instability until a crisis resets them | Quantified, tested on several agrarian states; generates measurable indicators | Fits agrarian societies best; modern application (e.g. *Ages of Discord*, 2016) is contested; risk of fitting cycles post hoc |
| **Political instability forecasting** (Goldstone et al. 2010) | A few structural variables (regime type and factionalism, infant mortality, neighbourhood conflict, discrimination) predict onset of instability | Out-of-sample testing reported; actionable | Predicts onset risk, not timing or outcome; regime categories carry judgment |
| **Diminishing returns to complexity** (Tainter 1988) | Societies solve problems by adding complexity (bureaucracy, specialization); marginal returns fall until simplification becomes rational | Explains why collapse can be adaptive | Hard to measure "returns"; underplays external shocks |
| **Institutions: inclusive vs extractive** (Acemoglu & Robinson 2012; *Narrow Corridor* 2019) | Inclusive political and economic institutions sustain growth; extractive ones block it; liberty lives in a "corridor" where state and society balance | Wide comparative scope | Critics cite reverse causality, vague categories, and cases of growth under extractive rule |
| **Violent levelling** (Scheidel 2017) | Large reductions in inequality historically followed mass-mobilization war, revolution, state collapse or pandemics | Long-run comparative data | Peaceful equalization cases debated; data quality varies |
| **Adaptive cycle and panarchy** (Holling 2001) | Systems cycle through growth, conservation, release and reorganization across nested scales | Useful for resilience and recovery thinking | Heuristic, hard to falsify |
| **Population and technology** (Kremer 1993) | Larger connected populations generate more ideas, accelerating growth (long-run) | Fits long-run macro data | Breaks down with demographic transition; tells little about specific polities |
| **Cultural evolution and demography** (Henrich 2004) | Small, isolated populations can lose complex skills | Formal model with clear threshold | Specific historical applications contested |
| **Preference falsification** (Kuran 1991) | Hidden opposition makes regimes look stable until a small trigger causes a sudden cascade | Explains surprise revolutions | Inherently hard to measure in advance |

## 3. Quantitative history

- **Seshat Global History Databank**: coded variables for hundreds of past polities. Turchin et al. (2018, PNAS) found that many measures of social complexity load on a single dominant dimension [E2]. Use for reference classes; check coding choices.
- **Secular cycles**: Turchin & Nefedov (2009) report recurring integrative and disintegrative phases of roughly two to three centuries in several agrarian states [E2, interpretation debated].
- **Track record**: structural models identify elevated risk, rarely the trigger or date. Treat any claim that a specific country is "due" for collapse in a given year as E4 at best.
- **Critiques**: selection of cases, coding subjectivity, overfitting cycles, neglect of contingency and ideas, and the "single dimension" being partly a result of how variables were chosen.
- **civlab**: `sdt` (stylized SDT), `polity` (asabiyyah-style frontier model), `knowledge` (Henrich). These are E3/E5 teaching models, not fitted reconstructions.

## 4. Case library (brief; verify details for any public deliverable)

| Case | Region, era | What changed | Leading explanations (debated) | Lesson for CIVITAS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Late Bronze Age collapse | Eastern Mediterranean, c. 1200 to 1150 BCE | Several palace states and trade networks failed within decades | Interlinked drought, earthquakes, invasions/migrations, trade disruption, systems interdependence | Interdependence turns local shocks into systemic cascades (`cascade`) |
| Western Roman Empire | Mediterranean/Europe, 3rd to 5th c. CE | Western imperial authority fragmented into successor kingdoms; the Eastern Empire continued until 1453 | Fiscal-military strain, civil wars, migrations, pandemics, climate; "fall" vs "transformation" debate | Collapse of a state is not collapse of a society |
| Han dynasty | China, 206 BCE to 220 CE | Unified empire fragmented into Three Kingdoms | Land concentration, factional conflict at court, Yellow Turban rebellion (184 CE), regional warlords | SDT-like pattern: elite conflict + popular immiseration + fiscal weakness |
| Classic Maya | Southern lowlands, 8th to 9th c. | Many royal cities depopulated; northern centres and Maya people continued | Drought (paleoclimate evidence), warfare, elite competition, environmental strain | Climate stress acts through political and economic structures |
| Abbasid Caliphate | Southwest Asia, 9th to 10th c. | Central authority fragmented; regional dynasties ruled under nominal caliphs; Baghdad sacked 1258 | Fiscal base erosion, military slave elites, provincial autonomy | Fragmentation can coexist with cultural flourishing |
| Angkor | Southeast Asia, 14th to 15th c. | Capital and hydraulic network declined; centre shifted | Monsoon variability and megadroughts (tree-ring evidence), infrastructure strain, trade shifts | Over-engineered infrastructure can become a rigidity |
| Mali Empire | West Africa, 13th to 16th c. | Trade-rich empire contracted; Songhai rose | Succession disputes, shifting trans-Saharan trade, rival states | Trade-route dependence; succession institutions matter |
| Inca | Andes, 15th to 16th c. | Rapid expansion, then civil war and Spanish conquest (1532) | Succession war, epidemic disease preceding conquest, extractive integration of conquered peoples | Exogenous disease shocks plus internal division |
| Ming to Qing | China, 1640s | Ming fell to rebellions and Manchu conquest | Fiscal crisis, silver flows, Little Ice Age cooling, famine, rebellion (Goldstone's case) | Global monetary and climate links in "local" crises |
| Tokugawa Japan | Japan, 1603 to 1868 | Long peace, then reconfiguration into the Meiji state | Fiscal strain, impoverished samurai elite, external pressure | A crisis can end in rapid institutional reinvention, not collapse |
| Ottoman Empire | Anatolia, Balkans, Middle East, c. 1300 to 1922 | Six centuries with repeated adaptation | Historians now reject a simple "decline" story after 1600; transformation and reform | Beware narratives that read the end into the middle |
| Norse Greenland | North Atlantic, c. 985 to 15th c. | Settlements abandoned | Cooling, trade decline (walrus ivory), isolation, adaptation limits (debated) | Small isolated populations face compound risks |
| Soviet Union | Eurasia, 1989 to 1991 | Rapid dissolution largely unforeseen by observers | Economic stagnation, nationalism, reform dynamics, preference falsification | Surprise is structural: hidden preferences cascade |

## 5. Diagnostic dashboard (for real or fictional polities)

| Pressure | Indicators (examples) | civlab proxy |
|---|---|---|
| Popular immiseration | real wages, food prices, youth bulge, urbanization | `sdt` wage, MMP |
| Elite overproduction / conflict | aspirants vs positions, elite income, factional splits | `sdt` elite_share, relative_elite_income |
| State fiscal distress | debt/output, revenue capacity, military spending share | `sdt` debt_ratio |
| Legitimacy and trust | compliance, protest, institutional trust surveys | `society` legitimacy |
| Cohesion | cross-group cooperation, polarization, secession movements | `society` group_cooperation_gap, `polity` solidarity |
| External pressure | neighbours' power, trade dependence, migration | `polity` |
| Capability and knowledge | skilled workforce, infrastructure maintenance backlog | `knowledge` |
| Systemic coupling | dependence on single routes or suppliers | `cascade` |
| Shock exposure | climate variability, disease, price volatility | shock parameters, `ews` on series |

## 6. Running a HISTORY analysis

1. Define what "rise", "decline" or "collapse" means for this case (territory, population, complexity, institutions, living standards).
2. Lay out the timeline (E1) separately from interpretations (E2).
3. Apply two or more theories from section 2; say what each predicts and where it fits or fails.
4. Compare with cases from other regions in section 4.
5. If modelling, choose the matching civlab model, document assumptions, and present results as E3 "under this model".
6. End with what evidence would discriminate between explanations.
